A relaxed walk-through of MyChart for the first-time user

What the screens mean, where the messages live, and how to actually find your lab results without three phone calls to the office.

By Michael McIntosh·Sep 16, 2026·8 min read

At the end of your last doctor's visit, someone at the front desk probably handed you a printout and said, "Your results will be in MyChart." Then they smiled like that settled everything. If you walked out to the car wondering what MyChart is, where it lives, and how you're supposed to get inside it, you are in very good company. Millions of people have a MyChart account they've never opened, and plenty more opened it once, felt lost, and went back to calling the office.

I want to change that, and I'll tell you why I care. I spent years working in healthcare IT, on the side of the counter where these systems get built and installed. I've also spent years as a physical therapist assistant, on the side where patients are trying to make sense of what they've been told. MyChart sits right between those two worlds. Used well, it's one of the most useful tools you'll ever have for your own health. You just need someone to walk you through the rooms before you have to find them in a hurry.

First, what MyChart actually is

MyChart is a patient portal, which is a fancy way of saying it's a private website and app that shows you a slice of your own medical record. It's made by a company called Epic, whose software runs the records at a huge number of hospitals and clinics across the country. That's why you might hear the name from your cardiologist, your hospital, and your eye doctor all at once.

Here's the part that confuses almost everyone: each health system runs its own MyChart. The app looks the same, but your account with one hospital is separate from your account with another. When you open the MyChart app for the first time, it asks you to choose your health system by name. Choose the one that told you about MyChart. If you see doctors in two different systems, you'll likely have two logins. Many versions of the app let you link those accounts so you can see both from one place, and it's worth asking the front desk about it at your next visit.

Getting in the door

There are usually two ways in. The first is an activation code, often printed on your after-visit summary (the pages they hand you as you leave). You type that code into the app or website, confirm your date of birth, and create a username and password. The second is signing up online without a code, where you answer a few questions to prove you are who you say you are. Either way works. If you get stuck, the health system's MyChart help line exists for exactly this, and calling it is not a failure. It's what the number is there for.

When you choose your password, write it down somewhere safe at home, or better yet, let your phone save it for you. You'll also probably be offered the chance to log in with your face or fingerprint on your phone. Say yes. It's secure, and it means you won't be locked out the one morning you really need to check something.

The home screen, room by room

Once you're in, the home screen can look busy. Different health systems arrange it differently, but almost all of them have the same handful of rooms. You'll usually find a row of buttons or tiles near the top, and a Menu button (often three short lines in a corner) that holds everything else. Here's the trick that saves the most frustration: the Menu has a search bar. If you can't find something, open the Menu and type what you're looking for, like "test results" or "bills." It works far better than hunting.

The rooms you'll use most are Messages, Test Results, Visits (or Appointments), Medications, and Billing. Let's take them one at a time.

Messages: your doctor's office, without the hold music

The Messages section is an inbox, a lot like email, but private and connected to your care team. Notes from your doctor's office show up here, along with reminders and sometimes a short comment about your results. You can also start a new message, often by choosing a button labeled something like "Ask a question" or "Message my care team." The app then asks you who you'd like to send it to, usually from a list of the doctors you've seen.

This is the room that replaces most of those phone calls. Need a refill? Wondering whether that new rash is related to the new pill? Want to ask whether you still need to fast before Friday's appointment? Send a message. Write it the way you'd say it out loud, one topic per message, and include the important details up front.

Two honest cautions from someone who's seen how these messages get handled on the other end. First, replies are not instant. Most offices aim to answer within a business day or two, and a nurse may respond before the doctor does. Second, MyChart is never the place for an emergency. Chest pain, trouble breathing, signs of a stroke, or anything that frightens you right now calls for 911, not a message.

Test Results: the room everyone is looking for

This is the big one. Your lab work, imaging reports, and other tests land in a section usually called Test Results. You'll find it on the home screen or by searching the Menu. Tap it and you'll see a list, newest at the top. Tap any result to see the details.

Now, here is something important that nobody tells you at the front desk. Because of a federal rule that took effect in 2021, many results are released to you as soon as they're ready, which can be before your doctor has looked at them. That rule exists for a good reason: it's your information, and you have a right to see it. But it means you may be reading a result with no explanation attached, on a Saturday, with the office closed until Monday.

So let me give you the reading lesson I'd give my own family. Each lab result usually shows your number, a normal range (sometimes called the reference range), and a flag if your number falls outside that range. A flag is a note, not a diagnosis. Normal ranges are built from large groups of people, and plenty of healthy people land a little high or a little low on one value. Labs that are a touch outside the range are extremely common and often mean nothing on their own. What matters is the pattern, your history, and what your doctor makes of it all.

If something worries you, write down the name of the test and your question, then send it as a message. Try not to spend the weekend searching the internet for every flagged number. The internet doesn't know you. Your doctor does. And if a result truly needs urgent attention, the office will call you rather than wait for you to find it in the app.

Visits, Medications, and Billing

The Visits or Appointments section shows what's coming up and what's already happened. Upcoming visits often have an eCheck-in button that lets you confirm your insurance and answer health questions from your couch, which can save you fifteen minutes on a clipboard in the waiting room. Past visits usually include the after-visit summary, so you can reread what the doctor said when you're calmer and home. That alone is worth the price of admission, especially if the appointment went quickly or the news was a lot to take in.

The Medications section shows the list your doctor's office has on file. Look it over once in a while. If it lists something you stopped taking last year, or misses something you take every day, tell the office. That list gets used when doctors make decisions, so a correct list is a safer list. You can often request refills from this screen too.

Billing, as you might expect, shows statements and lets you pay. If a bill doesn't make sense, it's perfectly fine to send a message to the billing office from right there.

Letting family help, the right way

Many people want a son, daughter, or spouse to help keep an eye on things. MyChart has a proper way to do this called proxy access. Instead of sharing your password (which can cause real headaches, and in some cases breaks the health system's rules), you grant your helper their own login that can see your account. You decide how much they see, and you can take it away at any time. Ask the front desk or the MyChart help line how your health system sets it up. It's usually a short form.

Your first ten minutes

If you've read this far, here's a simple first visit you can do tonight. Log in. Open the Menu and find the search bar. Search "test results" and look at the most recent one, reading the range and any flags. Then open Messages and read whatever is sitting there. That's it. You've toured the two most important rooms in the building.

The real gift of MyChart isn't convenience, although it is convenient. It's that you get to walk into your next appointment already knowing what's in your chart, with your questions written down. In my experience, that is the patient a doctor listens to most closely, and you're about ten minutes away from being that patient.
