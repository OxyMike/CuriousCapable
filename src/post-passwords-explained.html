Passwords, gently explained (and why "Buster1995!" is doing fine)

The truth about password rules, the case for a password manager, and how to set one up in fifteen forgiving minutes.

By Michael·Aug 26, 2026·6 min read

Somewhere out there is a person whose password is the name of their first dog, the year they got him, and an exclamation point because a website insisted on one. Maybe that person is you. If so, I have good news and I have honest news, and I'll give you both, because you deserve the whole picture and not a lecture.

The good news first. "Buster1995!" is not the disaster the internet would have you believe. It's eleven characters long, it mixes letters, numbers, and a symbol, and it isn't one of the handful of passwords (like "password" or "123456") that criminals try first. For a single account, it holds up better than you'd think. Nobody is sitting in a dark room trying to guess the name of your 1995 golden retriever.

Now the honest news. Buster is only doing fine on one condition: he lives on one website. If Buster1995! is also your email password, your bank password, and your Amazon password, then Buster is working far too hard, and that's where the real danger lives.

The rules you were taught are mostly outdated

For years, websites made us follow rules that felt like a punishment. Use a capital letter. Use a number. Use a symbol. Change it every ninety days. Don't write it down. The result was passwords that were hard for people to remember and surprisingly easy for computers to guess, because everyone made the same predictable moves (capital letter first, number and symbol at the end, sound familiar?).

The security experts have since changed their tune. The federal guidelines that many organizations follow now say that length matters more than odd characters, and that forcing people to change passwords on a schedule mostly makes things worse. A long, plain phrase like "porch swing lemonade thunderstorm" is much harder for a computer to crack than "Tr0ub4dor!" and far easier for you to remember. So if those old rules made you feel bad at passwords, the rules were the problem, not you.

The real threat is reuse

Here's how most accounts actually get broken into, and it has nothing to do with clever guessing. Every so often, some company gets hacked, and a list of its customers' email addresses and passwords ends up for sale. Criminals then take that list and try each email and password combination on hundreds of other websites, automatically, in minutes. If you used the same password at the hacked store and at your bank, they just walked through your front door with a key you handed to the store.

That's why one unique password per account matters so much more than how fancy any single password is. The trouble, of course, is that nobody can remember eighty different passwords. Which brings us to the tool I wish everyone had.

The case for a password manager

A password manager is a secure, locked notebook that lives on your phone and computer. It remembers all your passwords for you, fills them in when you log in, and creates new strong ones whenever you need them. You remember one thing: how to unlock it (usually your phone's passcode, your face, or your fingerprint). It does the rest.

If you've ever had your iPhone offer to "use a strong password" when signing up for something, you've already met one. Apple's is built right in, in an app simply called Passwords, on newer iPhones, iPads, and Macs. Android phones have Google Password Manager built in too. If your household mixes Apple and Android or Windows, a free option like Bitwarden works across all of them. Any of these is a giant step up from a sticky note or a single reused password, and the built-in one on the phone you already own is a perfectly good place to start.

I know the worry: "If all my passwords are in one place, isn't that one place a target?" It's a fair question. The answer is that these vaults are heavily encrypted, which means they're scrambled into nonsense that only your phone can unscramble. Even the company that makes the manager can't read your passwords. Reusing one password across dozens of websites is far riskier.

Fifteen forgiving minutes

Here is a gentle way to begin. You don't need to fix everything tonight, and you can't break anything by trying.

On an iPhone, open the Passwords app (it's a gray icon with a key). You'll likely see that it has already been saving passwords for you as you've logged into things over the years. Look for a section called Security. This is the helpful part: it shows you which passwords you've reused and which ones have shown up in a known data breach. Don't be alarmed by the list. Almost everyone's is long.

Now fix just three accounts, in this order. First, your email, because email is the master key to everything else (it's where password reset links go). Second, your bank. Third, the Apple or Google account your phone uses. For each one, go to that website or app, choose Change Password, and when your phone offers a strong password, say yes. It will save it automatically. You'll never need to type it yourself.

That's your fifteen minutes. Over the coming weeks, whenever you log into another site, let the phone suggest a new password and save it. You'll be fully updated before you know it, without ever sitting down for a dreaded marathon.

One more lock on the door

For those same three accounts, turn on two-step verification if it isn't on already. That's the feature that sends a code to your phone when you log in from somewhere new. Even if a criminal somehow gets your password, they can't get in without your phone in hand. And remember the golden rule: that code is for you to type, never to read aloud to someone who calls you.

As for Buster, let him retire with honor. Keep him for one low-stakes website you're fond of, or let the password manager replace him entirely. The bigger point is that good security isn't about being clever. It's about having a system you'll actually use, and you can start that system today with three accounts and a cup of coffee.
