What your bank will never ask you (print this)

A one-page list to keep next to the phone. If a caller asks any of these questions, hang up. No apology needed.

By Michael·Sep 30, 2026·4 min read

The phone rings at 4:45 on a Thursday afternoon. The caller ID says the name of your bank. The man on the line is polite, a little rushed, and very concerned. Someone in another state just tried to use your debit card at an electronics store, he says, and he's already stopped the charge. He just needs to confirm a few things so he can lock your account before they try again. Your heart rate goes up. That's the point. Scams don't work by fooling dumb people. They work by making smart people feel urgent, and urgent people skip the step where they think.

So here is a different approach. Instead of trying to spot a liar in real time (which is hard for anyone, at any age, with an adrenaline spike), you decide ahead of time what a real bank will never do. Then the call stops being a puzzle. If the caller asks for anything on the list below, you have your answer. You hang up. You don't have to be sure it's a scam. You don't have to be rude, and you don't have to be polite either. You just have to put the phone down.

The list: your bank will never ask you to

  1. Read back a code they just texted you. Those six digits are a lock on your account. Anyone asking for them is trying to open it.
  2. Tell them your PIN or your online banking password. Your bank never needs either one to help you. Not once, not for any reason.
  3. Move your money to a "safe account" to protect it. There is no such thing. A real bank protects your money where it already sits.
  4. Pay with gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency, or a payment app like Zelle to "fix" a problem. Banks don't collect anything in gift cards. Ever.
  5. Install an app or let them "connect" to your phone or computer. Remote access lets a stranger see your screen and, often, your accounts.
  6. Keep the call a secret from family or from the staff at your branch. A real bank is happy for you to call your daughter first.
  7. Act right now, before you can hang up and call back. Real fraud departments can wait five minutes while you dial the number on your card.

That's it. Seven lines. Print this post, cut out the list, and tape it to the wall beside the phone or slip it into your wallet behind your debit card. When a call goes sideways, you won't have to remember anything. You'll just have to look.

Why a list beats a gut feeling

I spent years in healthcare, and one thing hospitals do well is build checklists for the moments when people are most likely to make mistakes. A surgeon doesn't trust memory to confirm which knee is getting the operation. They check the list, every time, no matter how many years they've been doing it. Nobody thinks that makes the surgeon less capable. It makes them safer. A scam call is the same kind of moment. Your stress is high, the clock feels like it's ticking, and someone who sounds official is telling you what to do. That's exactly when a checklist earns its keep.

The list also takes the social pressure off. Many of us were raised to be courteous on the phone, to let people finish, and to assume good faith. Those are lovely qualities, and scammers count on them. Having a rule written down gives you permission to break the politeness habit. You aren't being rude. You're following the list.

What to do after you hang up

Once the phone is down, take a breath. Then flip over your debit or credit card and call the number printed on the back. Don't use a number the caller gave you, and don't hit redial, because some scammers can stay on the line or route the callback. If you're worried, a fresh call to the number on your card, or a walk into your local branch, will tell you in a few minutes whether anything is actually wrong with your account. Most of the time, nothing is.

If you did share something before you caught on, you haven't failed. Smart, careful people get caught every day because these callers are professionals. Call your bank right away, tell them exactly what you shared, and ask them to lock your card and change your online login. The faster you call, the more they can do. Then tell someone you trust. Scammers love silence and shame, and both of those make a bad situation worse.

One last thing about caller ID

The name on your screen is not proof of anything. Scammers can make caller ID show your bank's name and even its real phone number. It's called spoofing, and it's cheap and easy for them to do. So the name on the screen tells you who the caller wants you to think they are. Your list tells you what to do about it.

Print the list tonight, before you need it. You'll know it worked the first time a polite, worried voice asks for a code, and instead of a racing heart you feel something calmer: you already know the answer, and the answer is goodbye.
