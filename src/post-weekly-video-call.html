A weekly "phone call" that's actually a video call: a small ritual that changes things

One small Sunday tradition, why a face beats a voice, and how to start it this week without making it a production.

By Michael·Aug 12, 2026·4 min read

Picture a grandmother in Winter Haven and a nine-year-old in Ohio. They love each other. They also have absolutely nothing to say on the phone. Grandma asks how school is going. The answer is "good." She asks what he's been up to. The answer is "nothing." Two minutes later the phone is handed back to his mother, and everyone hangs up feeling vaguely like they failed a test nobody studied for.

Now picture the same two people on a video call. He holds his new lizard up to the camera, much too close, so all Grandma sees is a large green eyeball. She laughs. He laughs at her laughing. She asks the lizard's name, and suddenly there is a story about a pet store and an argument with his sister. Same people, same love, completely different call. The difference isn't the technology. It's that video gives everybody something to look at, and looking is where conversation starts, especially for kids.

Why a ritual beats a good intention

Most of us intend to video chat more. Intentions are wonderful, and they almost never survive a busy week. A ritual does, because it removes the decision. If Sunday at 4 o'clock is always the call, nobody has to wonder whether it's a good time, or who should call whom, or whether it's been too long and now it would be awkward. It just happens, like Sunday dinner used to.

There's a health reason, too, and I can't help bringing it up given my background. Researchers and public health leaders have spent the last several years sounding the alarm about loneliness, which is linked to real, physical health effects, not just a low mood. Regular, face-to-face contact (even through a screen) is one of the simplest protections we have. A weekly call isn't just sweet. It's good medicine.

How to set it up in one afternoon

Pick the time with the other household, not for them. Late Sunday afternoon works for many families because school and work are out of the way, but whatever fits their week is the right answer. Then make it repeat. On your phone's calendar, create the event once and set it to repeat every week, with a reminder fifteen minutes before. Now neither of you has to remember.

If you both have iPhones, FaceTime is the simplest choice: open your contacts, tap their name, and tap the video camera icon. If someone has an Android phone, you can still use FaceTime by sending them a FaceTime link, or you can use WhatsApp or Google Meet, which work on every phone. Choose one and stick with it so the routine never involves troubleshooting.

A few small touches make a big difference. Prop the phone up so your hands are free; a stack of books works fine. Sit facing a window or a lamp so the light falls on your face instead of behind your head. And don't worry about looking perfect. The grandchildren are not grading your lighting.

Give the call a little shape

The best rituals have one small thing that repeats. Ideas that work well with grandchildren include a weekly show-and-tell (each person brings one thing to the camera), reading one chapter of a book together over a few months, or one standing question, like "What was the best part of your week and the worst part?" With adult children, it might be a shared recipe you both try that week, or simply ten minutes with the coffee, no agenda required.

Keep it short. Fifteen minutes that leave everyone wanting more beat forty-five that drag. And when a child wanders off mid-call to chase the dog, let them go. That's not failure. That's a kid being a kid, with you along for the ride.

Schedule the first one before you close this page. You'll know it's working the week the other person reminds you it's almost time, because that's the moment a call becomes a tradition.
