Five free apps that handle medication reminders better than a pillbox

Honest pros and cons, including the one I'd point my own family to, written from years of healthcare-IT experience.

By Michael McIntosh·Aug 19, 2026·7 min read

Let me start by defending the pillbox, because it deserves it. That plastic box with the days of the week stamped on the lids is one of the most effective health tools ever invented. It costs a few dollars, it never needs charging, and a single glance tells you whether Tuesday's pills are still sitting there. If yours is working, keep it.

But a pillbox has one weakness, and it's a big one. It can't tap you on the shoulder. It sits quietly on the counter while you get absorbed in a phone call, a grandchild's visit, or a good book, and suddenly it's 9pm and you're not sure whether you took the afternoon dose. In my years working in healthcare IT, missed and doubled doses were among the most common and most preventable problems I saw. Not because people were careless, but because life is busy and memory is a lousy alarm clock at any age.

That's where a medication app earns its place. The best way to think about it is not as a replacement for your pillbox, but as the pillbox's assistant. The box holds the pills. The app does the remembering, keeps a record, and in some cases, gives you a heads-up before you run out. Here are five free options, with the honest trade-offs for each.

1. Apple Health's Medications feature (iPhone)

If you have an iPhone, you already own this one. It's tucked inside the Health app, the white icon with a red heart. Open Health, tap Search or Browse, and choose Medications. From there you add each medication by name (you can even point your camera at the pill bottle label on many phones), choose the dose, and set the schedule. When it's time, your phone reminds you, and you tap to log it as taken or skipped.

The good: It's free, private, built in, and has no ads. It can also warn you about some common interactions between medications you've entered. If you wear an Apple Watch, the reminders show up on your wrist, too.

The trade-offs: It only works on Apple devices, so it can't help a spouse with an Android phone. And it's built for straightforward schedules. Complicated routines, like a dose that tapers down over two weeks, can be awkward to set up.

2. MyTherapy (iPhone and Android)

MyTherapy is the app most often recommended as a free, full-featured option, and it earns that reputation. It handles reminders for as many medications as you need, and it also includes a simple health journal where you can log blood pressure, weight, blood sugar, or how you're feeling. You can create a report to bring to your doctor, which is genuinely useful when they ask, "How has it been going?" and your mind goes blank.

The good: Free, no limit on medications, works on both iPhone and Android, and the doctor reports are excellent.

The trade-offs: With all those extra features, there are more buttons and screens than you may want. Some versions now show ads to stay free. If all you want is a ding at 8am, it may be more app than you need.

3. Medisafe (iPhone and Android)

For years, Medisafe was the name everyone mentioned first, and it's still a polished, well-designed app with good interaction warnings and a feature that can alert a family member if you miss a dose. I want to be straight with you, though: in 2026, Medisafe moved most of its features behind a paid subscription. The free version now covers only a couple of medications.

The good: Excellent design, a strong reputation, and the family alert feature is a real comfort for some households.

The trade-offs: If you take more than two medications, you'll likely be asked to pay. That's not wrong of them, but it means Medisafe no longer belongs at the top of a free list. If you already use it and love it, the subscription may be worth it for you. If you're starting fresh, try one of the others first.

4. Samsung Health (Samsung Android phones)

If you have a Samsung Galaxy phone, you likely have Samsung Health already installed. It includes a medications section where you can add your prescriptions and set reminders, much like Apple's version.

The good: Free, no extra download, and it sits alongside the steps and sleep tracking you might already use.

The trade-offs: It's only for Samsung phones, and like Apple's tool, it's best for simple schedules. Other Android brands will want MyTherapy instead.

5. Your phone's plain old Reminders or Clock app

This one isn't fancy, and that's exactly the point. Every smartphone can set a repeating alarm or reminder. Name it something clear, like "Blood pressure pill, blue box," and set it to repeat daily.

The good: It's simple, reliable, and there's nothing new to learn. For one or two medications on a steady schedule, this may be all you ever need.

The trade-offs: It doesn't keep a record of what you actually took, so it can't answer the "did I or didn't I?" question later. It also won't warn you about interactions or refills.

The one I'd point my own family to

Here's my honest recommendation. If you have an iPhone and a fairly steady routine, start with the Medications feature in Apple Health. It's already on your phone, it's private, it's ad free, and it does the core job well. If you use an Android phone, or you're managing several medications and want a log to share with your doctor, choose MyTherapy.

Whichever app you pick, pair it with your pillbox rather than tossing the pillbox. Fill the box once a week, the way you always have, and let the app handle the reminding and the record. That way, if you ever wonder whether you took something, you have two ways to check: the empty compartment and the little checkmark on your phone.

A word on privacy and accuracy

When you put your medications into an app, you're sharing health information, so it's reasonable to ask who can see it. Apple's and Samsung's built-in tools keep the information tied to your own device and account. Free apps from other companies are generally careful, but read the privacy summary in the app store before you install, and skip any app that wants permissions that make no sense for a pill reminder.

And one gentle reminder from the healthcare side of my life: an app is a helper, not a pharmacist. Enter your medications exactly as they appear on the label. If anything about a dose, a timing, or an interaction warning confuses you, call your pharmacist. They are some of the most knowledgeable and underused people in all of healthcare, and they're happy to answer questions for free.

This week, pick one app and enter just one medication, the one you're most likely to forget. Let it remind you for seven days. You'll know it's working when you stop doing that little mental arithmetic at bedtime, and that freed-up worry is worth far more than the five minutes it took to set up.
